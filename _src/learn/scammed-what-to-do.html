If you're reading this because something just went wrong, take a breath and follow the steps in order. The first ten minutes matter most because attackers often return to drain anything left behind.

Step 1: Stop interacting

Close the suspicious website or app. Don't sign anything else, don't reply to the scammer, and don't send more money to “unlock” or “verify” anything.

Step 2: Work out what was exposed

What happenedWhat's at riskPriority action
You typed or shared your seed phraseEverything in that wallet, on every networkNew wallet + move all funds now
You signed an approval or permitThe specific tokens approvedRevoke approvals, then move those tokens
You sent funds to a scammerOnly what was sentDocument and report
You entered an exchange password/2FAYour exchange accountChange password, reset 2FA, contact the exchange
You installed software at someone's requestThe whole deviceDisconnect, use a clean device for recovery

Step 3: Revoke token approvals

On a clean device, open a reputable approval checker (such as revoke.cash — find it via our security links), connect the affected wallet and revoke any approvals you don't recognize, especially recent ones and unlimited allowances. Do this on every network you used. Revoking costs a small network fee, so you may need a little native currency (ETH, BNB, etc.) — but be careful: if your seed phrase is compromised, sweeper bots may steal any gas you send. In that case skip to step 4.

Step 4: Move remaining funds to a brand-new wallet

Create a new wallet on a clean device with a new seed phrase written offline. Transfer everything you can, starting with the most valuable assets. Never reuse a seed phrase that may have been exposed — not even for a “new” wallet created from the same words.

Step 5: Secure your accounts

  • Change passwords on exchanges and your email account — email is the key to resetting everything else.
  • Switch to app-based or hardware-key 2FA and revoke unknown sessions and API keys.
  • Contact your exchange's support through its verified website. If funds were sent to an exchange deposit address, the exchange may be able to freeze them after a law-enforcement request.
  • Scan your device for malware, or reset it.

Step 6: Document everything

Before anything disappears, save: the scam website address, screenshots of conversations, usernames and profile links, your wallet address, the scammer's addresses and every transaction hash (look them up on a block explorer). Write a short timeline while it's fresh.

Step 7: Report it

  • Chainabuse — the crypto community's multi-chain scam reporting database.
  • United States — the FBI's IC3 and the FTC's ReportFraud.
  • United Kingdom — Action Fraud.
  • Canada — the Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre, plus local police.
  • Australia — Scamwatch and ReportCyber.
  • Elsewhere — your national police cybercrime unit or consumer protection agency.
  • The platform where you met the scammer (social network, app store, search engine ad).
  • CoinLink — so we can warn others about the link.

Step 8: Avoid the second scam

“We can recover your crypto”

After a theft, victims are targeted by fake recovery firms, “ethical hackers” and even people impersonating police or lawyers. They ask for upfront fees, your seed phrase or remote access. Don't engage. Legitimate authorities don't charge victims, and no one can “hack back” a blockchain.

Looking after yourself

Being scammed is stressful and often embarrassing, but it happens to experienced people too — these operations are professional. Talk to someone you trust. Many countries have victim-support services that can help.

FAQ

Can a blockchain transaction be reversed?

No. Confirmed transactions are final. Recovery is only possible if funds reach a service that can freeze them, such as a regulated exchange or a stablecoin issuer, usually through law enforcement.

Should I pay a fee to withdraw from a platform that's holding my money?

No. Demands for taxes or fees before a withdrawal are a classic sign of a fake platform. Paying more only increases the loss.
