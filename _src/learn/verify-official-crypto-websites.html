Most crypto theft doesn't involve hacking a blockchain. It involves sending you to a website that looks exactly like the one you meant to visit. Clone sites copy the logo, layout and even the support chat of real exchanges, wallets and DeFi apps. Once you connect a wallet or type a recovery phrase, the money is gone — blockchain transactions can't be reversed.

The good news: a five-minute habit defeats almost every clone. This guide walks through it.

Why clone sites work so well

Clones succeed because they intercept you before you've had a chance to think. Common delivery routes include:

  • Search ads placed above the genuine result for searches like “metamask download” or “uniswap app”.
  • Replies and DMs from accounts posing as support staff after you post a question publicly.
  • Hacked official accounts posting a fake “claim” or “migration” link.
  • Emails and texts warning about a “suspicious login” with a link to “secure your account”.
  • QR codes and shortened links that hide the real destination.

In every case, the fix is the same: don't use the link you were given. Get to the site another way.

The 5-step verification method

  1. Start from a trusted source. Use a bookmark you created earlier, the address you typed from memory, or a reputable directory such as the CoinLink verified links. For a token or project, cross-check the website listed on a major data aggregator.
  2. Cross-check with a second source. The project's official documentation, its GitHub organization, or a long-standing official social account should list the same domain. Two independent sources that agree are strong evidence.
  3. Read the domain right-to-left. The real owner is the part just before the extension. In uniswap.org.app-claim.xyz, the true domain is app-claim.xyz, not Uniswap.
  4. Check for look-alike tricks (see the next section) and run anything suspicious through the link checker.
  5. Bookmark it. Once confirmed, create a bookmark and use it every time. This single habit beats clones permanently.

Signals that don't prove anything

  • The padlock. HTTPS certificates are free and automatic. Nearly all phishing sites have one.
  • A polished design. Clones copy the real HTML and images in minutes.
  • Being the top search result. Ads can outrank real results, and some clones even rank organically for a while.
  • Lots of followers. Follower counts and engagement can be bought.

Red flags once you're on a site

  • Any request for your seed phrase or private key. No legitimate site, wallet, exchange or support agent ever needs it.
  • A countdown or “limited eligibility” pressure to act right now.
  • A wallet prompt asking you to approve unlimited spending of a token you didn't intend to trade, or to sign a message you don't understand.
  • Download links for wallet apps that aren't from the official app stores via the official site.

The golden rule

If any page asks for your 12- or 24-word recovery phrase, close it. Nothing else on this list matters as much.

Make it automatic

Create a bookmarks folder named “Crypto — verified” and add your exchange, wallet and the apps you use. Use a separate browser profile for crypto with only your wallet extension installed. And when something urgent arrives by message, slow down: real platforms don't expire your account in 30 minutes.

FAQ

Is a website safe if it's not on any blocklist?

Not necessarily. New phishing domains appear every hour and may not be listed yet. Absence from a blocklist is weak evidence; matching a verified domain from two trusted sources is strong evidence.

Can I trust links in a project's official Discord or Telegram?

Usually, but those channels are frequent targets. Fake announcements are posted by compromised admin accounts or bots. Verify against the website listed in multiple places before acting on any announcement link.

What should I do if I already connected my wallet to a fake site?

Revoke token approvals immediately and move funds to a new wallet if you exposed your seed phrase. Our guide “Scammed? What to do next” covers every step.
