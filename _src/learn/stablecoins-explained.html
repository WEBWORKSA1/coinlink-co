Stablecoins are tokens designed to hold a steady value — most commonly one US dollar. They're the plumbing of crypto markets: traders park funds in them, DeFi apps lend them, and people use them to send dollars across borders in minutes. But “stable” describes a goal, not a guarantee.

The four main designs

1. Fiat-backed

A company holds reserves — cash, short-term government bonds and similar assets — and issues tokens against them. Eligible customers can redeem tokens for dollars. The largest stablecoins by supply use this model. Key questions: how good are the reserves, how often are they attested by an accountant, and who can redeem?

2. Crypto-collateralized

Users lock more crypto than the stablecoins they mint — for example $150 of ETH for $100 of stablecoins. If collateral value falls too far, positions are liquidated to protect the peg. This is transparent on-chain but depends on smart contracts and price oracles working under stress.

3. Synthetic / delta-neutral

Some newer designs hold crypto while shorting it through derivatives so that the combined position stays roughly flat in dollar terms, earning funding payments. They work well when markets are normal and can be strained when funding rates turn negative or exchanges fail.

4. Algorithmic

These try to hold the peg mainly through incentives and a sister token, with little external collateral. The 2022 collapse of TerraUSD, which wiped out tens of billions of dollars of value within days, showed how quickly confidence can unravel in such designs.

What can break a peg

  • Reserve problems — assets that aren't there, aren't liquid, or are stuck at a failed bank.
  • Bank runs — mass redemptions faster than reserves can be sold.
  • Smart-contract or oracle failures in collateralized designs.
  • Regulatory action that halts minting or redemption.
  • Liquidity gaps — on smaller exchanges a stablecoin can trade off-peg even if reserves are fine.

Freezes and censorship

Most fiat-backed stablecoin contracts let the issuer freeze specific addresses, typically in response to law enforcement or hacks. That can help recover stolen funds, but it also means your balance isn't as permissionless as native crypto.

Regulation is catching up

Several jurisdictions now have dedicated stablecoin rules covering reserves, audits and redemption rights — including the EU's MiCA framework and the United States' federal stablecoin legislation passed in 2025. Regulated issuers generally publish monthly reserve reports. Check whether a stablecoin is authorized where you live.

Using stablecoins safely

  1. Prefer stablecoins with frequent, independent reserve attestations and clear redemption terms.
  2. Verify the contract address — fake tokens with identical names and tickers are common. Use the issuer's verified site or a major data aggregator.
  3. Send on the correct network. A stablecoin on one chain is a different token from the same brand on another chain.
  4. Diversify across issuers if you hold significant amounts.
  5. Treat very high stablecoin “savings” yields as a risk signal — the yield comes from somewhere.

FAQ

Are stablecoins the same as a bank deposit?

No. They usually aren't covered by deposit insurance, and redemption may be limited to verified customers of the issuer.

Why does my stablecoin show $0.998 instead of $1?

Small deviations are normal market noise and fees. Sustained moves of several percent are a warning sign worth investigating.
