The Most Common Crypto Scams in 2026 — and the Red Flags

Phishing clones, fake airdrops, impersonated support, pig-butchering, giveaway streams, rug pulls, address poisoning and recovery scams — how each works and how to spot it.

By CoinLink.co Editorial · 11 min read · Updated 2026-11-19 · How we write

Key takeaways

  • Almost every crypto scam relies on urgency, secrecy or a promise of easy returns.
  • Wallet drainers trick you into signing approvals rather than stealing your keys.
  • Nobody legitimate will double your crypto, DM you first, or ask for your seed phrase.
  • “Recovery services” that contact victims are almost always a second scam.

Crypto scams change their costumes constantly, but the underlying scripts are few. Learn the scripts and you'll recognize the next variation even if you've never seen it before.

1. Phishing clone websites

How it works: a copy of a real exchange, wallet or DeFi app is promoted through search ads, social posts or emails. You log in or connect a wallet and the scammer captures your credentials or a malicious signature.

Red flags: you arrived via an ad or message; the domain has extra words or odd spelling; the site asks for your seed phrase. Defence: bookmarks and the link checker. See how to verify websites.

2. Fake airdrops and “claim” pages

How it works: you're told you're eligible for free tokens. The claim page asks you to connect and “sign to claim”. The signature is actually an approval or a permit that hands over your tokens or NFTs. Sometimes worthless tokens appear in your wallet with a website in their name, luring you to the drainer.

Red flags: unexpected eligibility; tokens you never bought; pressure to claim quickly. Defence: only claim airdrops from links on the project's verified site, and use a separate low-balance wallet.

3. Impersonated support

How it works: you post a question on social media or Discord and within minutes “support” replies or DMs you, offering help through a form or a call. They ask for your seed phrase, to “validate” or “sync” your wallet, or to install remote-access software.

Defence: real support never DMs first and never needs your seed phrase. Contact support only through the help page on the verified website.

4. Pig-butchering (relationship investment scams)

How it works: a friendly stranger — often met through a wrong-number text, dating app or social network — builds trust over weeks, then introduces a “trading platform” where you see impressive profits. When you try to withdraw, you're told to pay taxes or fees first. The platform is fake and the money is gone.

Red flags: an online friend steering you to a specific platform; returns that are too consistent; withdrawal blocked by fees. Defence: only use exchanges you found independently and verified; never pay to withdraw.

5. Giveaway and “double your crypto” scams

How it works: deepfake videos or hijacked accounts of famous founders promise to send back double whatever you send. Livestreams on video platforms often show a QR code to an address.

Defence: there are no exceptions to this rule — no one will double your crypto.

6. Rug pulls and honeypot tokens

How it works: a new token is hyped, buyers pile in, and the developers drain the liquidity pool or sell a large hidden allocation. “Honeypot” tokens are coded so you can buy but not sell.

Red flags: anonymous team with no track record, locked comments, unverified contracts, a huge share of supply in a few wallets. Defence: check contracts with security scanners and test that a token can be sold before investing more than a tiny amount.

7. Address poisoning

How it works: scammers send tiny transactions from an address whose first and last characters match one you use often. Later, you copy an address from your history and accidentally send funds to the look-alike.

Defence: use your address book or a fresh copy from the recipient, and check the full address — not just the start and end — for large transfers.

8. Fake apps, extensions and wallet updates

How it works: cloned wallet apps appear in app stores or as browser extensions, or pop-ups claim your wallet needs an urgent “update” that requires your recovery phrase.

Defence: install only via links on the official site; wallets never ask for your phrase to update.

9. Recovery scams

How it works: after you've been scammed, “asset recovery experts”, hackers or fake law firms contact you — often citing your public complaint — and charge upfront fees to recover your funds. They never deliver.

Defence: report losses to the authorities and Chainabuse, and ignore unsolicited recovery offers. Legitimate law enforcement doesn't charge victims fees.

The universal red flags

  • Urgency: countdowns, “last chance”, account suspension threats.
  • Secrecy: “don't tell anyone”, moving the conversation to private chat.
  • Guarantees: fixed daily returns, risk-free profits, doubling.
  • Requests for secrets: seed phrases, private keys, 2FA codes, remote access.
  • Unusual payment rails: paying “fees” in crypto or gift cards to unlock withdrawals.

FAQ

How do wallet drainers steal without my seed phrase?

They trick you into signing a transaction or off-chain permit that grants their contract permission to move your tokens. Reading prompts carefully and using a separate low-value wallet for unknown sites stops most drains.

Can stolen crypto be recovered?

Sometimes, if funds reach a regulated exchange that can freeze them quickly after a law-enforcement request. Report immediately; speed matters.

Where can I report a crypto scam?

Chainabuse for the crypto community, plus your national fraud reporting service — for example IC3 in the US, Action Fraud in the UK, the Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre or Scamwatch in Australia. You can also report scam links to CoinLink.
