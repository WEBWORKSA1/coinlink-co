Crypto Wallet Security: Seed Phrases, Hardware Wallets & Approvals

How to set up and protect a crypto wallet: seed phrase backups, hot vs cold wallets, hardware wallet buying rules, approvals and everyday habits.

By CoinLink.co Editorial · 10 min read · Updated 2026-11-19 · How we write

Key takeaways

  • Your seed phrase is your wallet — anyone who has it controls your funds.
  • Back it up offline in two places; never photograph or type it into a website.
  • Use a hardware wallet for long-term holdings and a separate hot wallet for experiments.
  • Review token approvals regularly and revoke what you no longer use.

A crypto wallet doesn't actually store coins. It stores the keys that prove you control coins recorded on a blockchain. Protecting those keys is the entire game. This guide covers the decisions that matter, in order of importance.

Custodial vs self-custody

When your crypto sits on an exchange, the exchange holds the keys — that's custodial. It's convenient and lets you reset a forgotten password, but you depend on the exchange's solvency and security. A wallet where only you hold the keys is self-custody: no one can freeze it, but no one can rescue you if you lose the keys either.

Many people use both: an exchange for buying and selling, and a self-custody wallet for holdings they plan to keep.

The seed phrase: treat it like the money itself

When you create a self-custody wallet, it shows a recovery phrase (seed phrase) of 12 or 24 words. Those words can recreate your wallet on any device. That's why they're both your backup and your biggest vulnerability.

  • Write it down by hand on paper, or stamp it into a metal backup plate that survives fire and water.
  • Store two copies in separate secure places, so a single fire, flood or theft doesn't end your access.
  • Never photograph it, store it in a notes app, email it to yourself, or save it in cloud storage. Malware and account breaches target exactly these places.
  • Never type it into a website or share it with “support”. Legitimate services never ask.
  • Consider a passphrase (sometimes called the 25th word) for extra protection on large balances — but understand that losing the passphrase means losing access.

Hot wallets vs cold wallets

Hot walletCold (hardware) wallet
Where keys liveOn an internet-connected phone or browserOn a dedicated offline device
Best forSmall balances, daily use, trying new appsLong-term holdings and large balances
Main riskMalware, malicious approvals, phishingLosing the device and the backup; supply-chain tampering

A practical setup: a hardware wallet as your “vault” and a separate hot wallet with a small balance as your “spending account” for new apps, mints and airdrops. If the hot wallet is ever drained, the vault is untouched.

Buying a hardware wallet safely

  • Buy directly from the manufacturer's official website (check our verified wallets list) or an authorized reseller.
  • Never use a device that arrives with a pre-written seed phrase or that's already set up. Always generate a new phrase yourself.
  • Install companion apps only from links on the manufacturer's official site.
  • Verify addresses on the device screen when receiving funds — malware can swap addresses on your computer.

Token approvals: the silent risk

On Ethereum and similar networks, using a DeFi app often requires an approval that lets a smart contract move a specific token from your wallet. Many apps request unlimited approvals for convenience. If that contract is later exploited — or if the “app” was a phishing site — the approval can be used to drain that token without any further signature from you.

Two habits fix most of this: read wallet prompts before confirming (watch for “approve”, “permit”, “increase allowance” and “setApprovalForAll”), and periodically review and revoke approvals with a reputable tool. Our guide on token approvals goes deeper.

Everyday habits that prevent most losses

  1. Bookmark your wallet and the apps you use; never connect through links in messages.
  2. Send a small test transaction before large transfers.
  3. Check the first and last 6 characters of every pasted address to defeat clipboard malware and address poisoning.
  4. Keep your operating system, browser and wallet software updated.
  5. Use a dedicated browser profile for crypto with no unnecessary extensions.
  6. Be wary of anyone who contacts you first — especially “support” and new online friends with investment tips.

Plan for the unexpected

Write simple instructions for a trusted person explaining that you hold crypto, which wallets you use and where your backups are — without writing the seed phrase into those instructions. Many families never recover crypto because no one knew it existed.

FAQ

Is it safe to keep crypto on an exchange?

It can be reasonable for amounts you actively trade, on a reputable, regulated exchange with strong 2FA. For long-term holdings, self-custody removes the risk of exchange insolvency or freezes.

What happens if I lose my hardware wallet?

Your funds are safe as long as you still have your seed phrase. Restore it on a new device. If someone else finds the device, they still need your PIN.

Can someone steal crypto with just my wallet address?

No. An address is public, like an account number. They need your keys, your seed phrase, or a token approval you signed.
